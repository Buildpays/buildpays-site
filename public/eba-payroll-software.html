AEBA payroll

EBA payroll software that gets the agreement right

Generic payroll software assumes a 38-hour week, one hourly rate per employee, superannuation as a flat percentage, and allowances typed in by hand. Under a CFMEU Victorian Construction and General Division enterprise agreement, every one of those assumptions is wrong — and each one fails quietly, the same way, every week.

BBreaks

Where generic payroll breaks under a construction EBA

None of what follows is exotic. It is the ordinary weekly reality of running a crew under a construction agreement, and it is the part off-the-shelf payroll was never built for.

The ordinary week is 36 hours, not 38

This is the single most consequential default in the whole system. A payroll engine that treats 38 hours as the point where overtime starts will pay hours at ordinary rates that the agreement says are already at penalty rates. It is a small amount per worker per week, which is precisely why it survives. It is also completely systematic — the same error, for every worker, every week, going back as far as the configuration does. That is the difference between a payroll mistake and a back-pay claim.

RDOs are a cycle, not a leave balance

Rostered days off under a construction agreement run on a calendar, accrued through ordinary hours worked and taken on set days. Generic payroll has no concept of this. In practice the RDO ends up either unpaid, or paid out of an annual leave balance it should never have touched, or keyed in manually every cycle by whoever remembers. The calendar is also reissued each year, so a system with the dates hardcoded is wrong from the first cycle of the new one — and nobody gets told, because the software has no way to know.

Daily hire is a real employment type

Most payroll systems offer full-time, part-time and casual. Daily hire is none of those, so it gets mapped to whichever is closest and the differences — notice, termination, the specific entitlements the agreement attaches to daily hire employment — get handled by hand, or not at all. Anything handled by hand every fortnight is something that will eventually be handled wrong.

Allowances are conditional, not standing lines

Allowances under the agreement depend on facts about the particular day: which site, what work, what conditions, how far the worker travelled. Entering them manually produces both failure modes at once — missed on days they were owed, and applied on days they were not.

Travel is the clearest example. As at 1 March 2026, the travel allowance under the CFMEU Victorian construction and general wage sheets is $57.60 per day, paid per day on the conditions the agreement sets, not as a flat weekly line. Software that cannot evaluate a per-day condition cannot get this right except by luck.

Superannuation has a floor, not just a percentage

As at 1 March 2026, superannuation is the greater of $280 per week or 12% of ordinary time earnings. That word “greater” is doing an enormous amount of work. A payroll system that only knows how to multiply ordinary time earnings by a percentage will silently underpay every worker in every week where 12% of their ordinary time earnings comes to less than $280 — which is exactly the short weeks: a starter’s first part-week, a week with unpaid absence, a week broken up by weather. Those are the weeks nobody goes back and re-checks.

The common thread. Every one of these failures is silent, systematic and repeated. No worker notices two hours at the wrong rate in one week. Everyone notices it across a year, and by then it is a claim covering the whole crew for the whole period.

CMust do

What EBA-correct payroll software actually has to do

  1. Hold the agreement as rules, not as rates. Storing a rate per employee is not configuring an agreement. The ordinary-hours threshold, the overtime steps, the RDO cycle and the conditions attached to each allowance all have to be expressed as logic the system evaluates, not numbers a person maintains.
  2. Classify hours automatically from real start and finish times. Workers should enter when they started and when they finished. Deciding which of those hours are ordinary, which are overtime and at what step is the software’s job. A worker choosing their own pay category is a system that will be wrong.
  3. Run the RDO calendar as a calendar. Accrual, the scheduled day, and paying it without anyone submitting anything — with the ability to load a new calendar each year.
  4. Apply allowances conditionally, per day. Driven by the site, the work and the conditions actually recorded for that day.
  5. Calculate superannuation as a comparison. Work out both the weekly floor and the percentage of ordinary time earnings, then pay the greater. Every week, per worker, not as a quarterly check.
  6. Show its working. When a worker or a delegate asks why a week paid what it did, you need the breakdown — hours, classification, rule applied — not a single net figure.
  7. Be reconfigurable when rates change. Wage sheets are updated. A system that requires a vendor ticket to reflect a rate change will be out of date for as long as the ticket takes.

EAsk

What to ask any payroll vendor

  • What do you treat as the ordinary weekly hours, and where is that configured?
  • How do you handle the RDO cycle, and what happens when next year’s calendar is issued?
  • Is daily hire a supported employment type, or is it mapped onto something else?
  • Can an allowance be conditional on the site and the day, or only set per employee?
  • Do you calculate superannuation as the greater of a weekly floor and a percentage?
  • Can you show me the rule that produced a given line on a given payslip?

A vendor who cannot answer these is not necessarily selling bad software. They are selling software built for a different kind of employer.
