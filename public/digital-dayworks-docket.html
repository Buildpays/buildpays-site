The digital dayworks docket that ends at the pay run

A dayworks docket has to carry three things: the labour hours per worker, the plant and equipment used, and the materials consumed — and then get countersigned by the head contractor on site. Most docket apps stop the moment that signature lands. That is the easy half. The hard half is turning it into hours your workers get paid correctly for, and a variation your bookkeeper can actually bill. PayKicker does both from the one capture — docket to pay and docket to invoice, each in one click to Xero or MYOB.

What a dayworks docket actually has to capture

A dayworks docket — a day docket, a T&M sheet, a variation sheet, whatever your head contractor calls it — is the record of work done outside your fixed-price scope. It is also the only evidence you will have when that work is queried three months later by someone who was not on site. If it does not carry all of the following, it will not survive the claim:

  • Date, site, and where on the site. “Level 3 core, east face” is worth something. The project name on its own is not.
  • The instruction. Who directed the work, at what time, and on whose authority. A name and a timestamp, not “as instructed”.
  • Every worker by name, with their classification and their real hours. Start, finish and breaks — per person, not a crew total.
  • Plant, hire and materials with quantities. Pumps, scaffold, props, concrete.
  • A description a stranger can follow, written on the day rather than reconstructed on Friday afternoon from memory.
  • A countersignature from someone with authority, captured on site before knock-off.
  • Photos. The obstruction, the condition, the extra work — timestamped and attached to the docket rather than sitting in someone’s camera roll.

The signature is the part everyone remembers. The hours per worker per classification is the part that quietly decides whether you made money on the variation, because it is the only field that has to be right twice — once for the client, and once for your own pay run.

Capture labour, plant and materials on every docket

PayKicker captures all three on the one docket, on site, from the worker’s phone. Labour goes on per worker, by name and classification, with real start, finish and break times. Plant and materials go on as a separate list — the pump, the scaffold, the props, the concrete — picked from a price list the office maintains for that project, with the worker entering only the quantity and unit. Anything not on the list goes on as a free-text line and is flagged in the cost report so the office can price it, rather than being left off the docket and never billed.

The signed docket shows what was used and how much, never a rate. The rates live with the office and are applied when the client cost report is built, so the crew never sees your pricing and the head contractor signs for quantities they can check on the day.

How paper dockets leak revenue

Paper rarely fails dramatically. It leaks, in four predictable places.

The docket that never gets signed

The leading hand does the work, means to get it signed, and the supervisor has gone by the time he gets to the gate. Nobody chases it that night. A week later you are asking a head contractor to sign for hours he cannot verify, and he is entirely within his rights to decline. That work is now free.

The docket that gets signed and then lost

Signed at 3pm, folded into a hi-vis pocket, through the wash on Sunday. Or it reaches the office and goes into a tray with forty others, and the one that matters surfaces after the progress claim has already gone out.

The docket that gets signed for the wrong hours

Written up at the end of the day from memory, so the numbers get rounded to something tidy. Rounding down is money you have already spent on wages and will never bill. Rounding up is worse: it is the docket the head contractor produces the next time you claim anything at all.

The docket that never reaches payroll

This is the expensive one, and it is invisible. The docket gets signed, gets scanned, gets invoiced — and the hours of overtime it represents are entered into payroll separately, by a different person, from a different piece of paper. When those two records disagree, you have either underpaid a worker under your enterprise agreement or billed a client for hours you never paid out. Both get found later, by someone who is not pleased.

Docket to pay, and to invoice

Plenty of apps will get a docket signed on a phone. That addresses the first three leaks and leaves the fourth exactly where it was, because a docket-only tool has no idea what any of those hours cost you. It captures ten hours. It cannot tell you that two of them were overtime at a penalty rate, that the day attracted a travel allowance, or that one of the workers on it is a daily hire employee whose entitlements differ from the rest of the crew.

PayKicker treats the docket as a payroll document that happens to also be a billing document. The same captured hours run through your enterprise agreement rules, so the docket carries a real cost as well as a claim value, and those same hours land in the pay run in Xero or MYOB without anyone retyping them. Once the docket is signed, one click turns it into the invoice, with the signed sheet attached.

The practical test. Ask any docket tool what a signed docket cost you in wages. If it cannot answer, you are still doing that reconciliation by hand every fortnight — and that reconciliation is where the margin on dayworks quietly disappears.

It becomes the claim. The signature, the photos and the description are locked to the docket together with the time they were captured, so the variation is evidenced rather than argued.

It becomes the timesheet. Those hours are the same hours that go to payroll. The worker does not fill in a docket and then a timesheet, and the office does not key the same day twice.

It becomes the cost. Your agreement’s rules are applied to the hours automatically, so the docket shows what the work is worth to bill and what it cost to pay, side by side, on the day it happened rather than at the end of the month.

What changes in the office

The change most office managers notice first is not the app. It is that the Friday afternoon spent chasing paper stops. Unsigned dockets are visible while they can still be signed, not after. Hours arrive already classified against the agreement instead of as a number somebody has to interpret. The progress claim gets built from records that are already reconciled to the pay run, because they are the same records.

The second change is slower. Once every docket carries a cost as well as a claim, you can see which sites, which head contractors and which kinds of dayworks actually make money — which is not always the ones people assume.
